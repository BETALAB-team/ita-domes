Identification of Italian user archetypes for building energy simulations Application of the corrected framework to the 2021 national survey Research manuscript prepared for review. Adapted from Khajedehi, Vivian and Zarrella, Energy & Buildings 344 (2025), 115924 [1]. New calculations replace the source article's numerical results. This document does not represent an approved correction, a published article, or endorsement by the original co-authors. Abstract Occupant-related inputs in building energy simulations require a transparent link between household characteristics, dwelling information and energy-related observations. This study reanalyzes the 2021 Italian household energy survey using a corrected and reproducible version of an archetype-based framework. Of 54,069 released households, 52,632 satisfy the common restriction to one to five members and complete education information for members aged at least 15. Age-class midpoints are treated as proxies rather than observed ages. Survey-weighted principal component analysis and four-cluster k-means are fitted only to the 2013 training subset; the same transformations and centroids are used throughout the paired analyses. A multinomial model links dwelling-age and floor-area proxies to four probabilities that are jointly constrained to sum to one. On 6,954 held-out households with known dwelling categories, its multiclass Brier score is 0.7071, compared with 0.7289 for the training-marginal baseline. Training-derived energy groups are selected on a separate tuning subset and evaluated without further merging on the test subset. The heating model retains 8 groups and yields a test root mean squared error of 4.76 hours, compared with 5.08 hours for a constant predictor. Cooling ownership and operating hours are analyzed alongside insulation and window improvements during the previous five years. The improvement indicator enters the heating model. Appliance electricity is estimated from ownership and use with a verified 2013 component model and explicit assumptions for coarser 2021 inputs. The findings support a bounded, testable representation of household heterogeneity, but do not demonstrate causal behavioral effects or improved building-simulation accuracy. Sensitivity to coarse age and education inputs remains a central limitation. Keywords: household archetypes; occupant-related inputs; ISTAT; probabilistic modeling; survey weights; reproducibility; heating schedules. 1. Introduction The operation of residential buildings depends on more than envelope properties and technical-system efficiency. Occupancy, appliance use, temperature preferences and the timing of heating interact with the physical characteristics of the dwelling. Consequently, a simulation supplied with one generic household profile can differ from a simulation supplied with information about the actual residents. The broader literature on the energy performance gap and occupant behavior provides the motivation for studying these inputs [2,3]. It does not, by itself, establish that any particular demographic clustering improves prediction. National household surveys offer a practical source of demographic and equipment information at a scale that is rarely available in monitored-building studies. Their strengths are coverage, common questionnaires and documented coding. Their limitations include coarse categories, self-reporting, survey routing and incomplete information about the building and its operation. An archetype method must preserve those distinctions: a demographic label is not an observation of a behavioral mechanism, and a reported operating duration is not a measurement of delivered heat. 1.1. International standards Schedule-based standards provide reference inputs for energy calculations. ISO 18523-2:2018 specifies formats and categories for residential schedules and conditions, including occupancy, technical-system operation, appliance usage and internal gains [8]. Its publicly available scope identifies the included values as informative. EN 16798-1:2019 is the source of a second set of examples discussed by the original article [1,9]. These sources have different purposes from a fitted household prediction model. A difference between a reference schedule and a survey mean is therefore not sufficient evidence that the standard is erroneous. The corrected comparison retains the source article's interest in diversity, but removes unsupported claims about the motives or composition of standardization committees. It also separates appliance electricity from heat released within occupied space. Electrical energy may be converted into sensible heat, latent heat, heat removed by water, or energy dissipated elsewhere. A model of appliance kWh cannot be inserted unchanged as an internal-gain schedule without assumptions about that allocation and its timing. 1.2. Literature review Reviews describe a wide range of occupant-modeling approaches, from fixed schedules to stochastic representations and explicit interactions with building systems [3]. Monitored occupant-behavior databases provide information about presence and interactions with equipment that is more direct than demographic variables [4]. Nevertheless, such observations are not routinely available for every dwelling in a building-stock model. Survey-derived archetypes may therefore be useful as uncertain inputs when detailed information is absent. Studies of alternative occupancy-profile assumptions show that schedule selection can materially change simulated demand [5]. This motivates sensitivity analysis rather than a presumption that more archetypes necessarily produce a more accurate model. A useful archetype framework should provide a reproducible feature definition, a clearly identified training population, probabilities for unknown occupants, and an evaluation against simple alternatives. The present analysis addresses those requirements while retaining the source article's sequence of demographic clustering, dwelling association and energy-pattern analysis [1]. 1.3. Research gap and objectives Three questions guide this reanalysis. First, can a fixed four-archetype representation summarize the available household information under explicit preprocessing assumptions? Second, does dwelling information improve out-of-sample probability prediction beyond the overall archetype distribution? Third, do combinations of archetype, location and dwelling type improve prediction of the available energy-related response? The questions are associative and predictive. They do not ask whether archetype membership causes energy use, nor whether a resulting schedule has already reduced a building simulation's performance gap. The 2021 manuscript applies the frozen 2013 demographic model rather than independently redefining and renaming four new clusters. The manuscript retains corresponding figure types, coefficient tables, group summaries and discussion sections. Where the available evidence cannot support the original statistic or comparison, the replacement is stated explicitly rather than generating a visually similar but scientifically different result without explanation. 2. Dataset The 2021 research release contains 54,069 household records. ISTAT documents a mixed web/telephone collection strategy and a different sampling frame from the 2013 survey [7]. Equipment and dwelling questions describe the survey context, whereas the heating-hours questions refer to winter 2020. These periods must not be collapsed into an unqualified 2021 energy-demand observation. The released record identifier is used only within its own survey. No attempt is made to link individual households between years. The paired analyses are repeated cross-sections, not a panel. To define the same accessible household-size domain in both years, households with fewer than one or more than five members are excluded. The 2021 release supplies demographic fields for at most five members and top-codes its larger-household category. Each included member must have a valid age category; all members in categories beginning at age 15 or above must also have a known education category. Children are not assigned missing adult education values of zero. These rules retain 52,632 households and exclude 1,437. Exclusion reasons are exported by record identifier. This complete-case domain is not the entire national household population. The official household expansion weights are retained. They define weighted means, distributions, PCA, clustering and probability-model losses. People-weighted archetype shares multiply each household weight by its recorded number of members. Reported household counts remain actual sample counts, not rounded expansion totals. Because the analysis does not reconstruct the survey's complete variance-estimation design, its standard errors and tests are explicitly model-based; using expansion weights is not a substitute for strata, primary sampling units or replicate weights. Unknown construction period accounts for 14.07% of the eligible household weight. Such records remain in the demographic analysis but are omitted when a dwelling-age predictor is required. The dwelling model therefore describes a conditional sample whose composition may differ from that of households with an unknown construction year. The 2021 appliance-electricity response is constructed from survey ownership and use using the main-branch appliance model. Its component boundary reproduces all 20,000 saved 2013 household totals to numerical precision: lighting, small appliances, CE refrigerators/freezers, CE washing/drying/dishwashing, TVs/computers and standby. Cooking, space heating, hot water and cooling electricity are excluded. This is a modeled annual reference estimate, not a metered observation or an expenditure conversion. Building classifications also require semantic harmonization. Both releases distinguish single-family and multi-family dwellings, but their apartment subcategories do not describe the same attribute. The common classification therefore uses single-family, multi-family and apartment. Geographic divisions are consolidated into north-west, north-east, centre, and south/islands. The 2013 subjective insulation rating is retained for that wave. In 2021, v3_14gh_new records insulation of walls, ceilings or floors and/or replacement doors, windows or frames in the last five years. This renovation measure is analyzed explicitly as insulation/window improvements; it does not measure current thermal performance. The comparable 2013 renovation items are q_3_19_7 and q_3_19_8, rather than q_3_17. 3. Methods Figure 1 summarizes the corrected workflow. All record-level decisions precede model fitting. Regression analysis and energy groups The heating response is the sum of reported operation in three eight-hour daily bands. To respect the differing question routes, both years are restricted to use-frequency categories 1 or 2, with all three hours observed and each value between 0 and 8. Missing hours are not zeros. The resulting mean describes a typical reported winter day among frequent users; it is neither an annual heating duration nor the mean over all households. The 2021 annual appliance-kWh response is modeled using the verified component boundary and the assumptions documented below. For correspondence with the original ANOVA table, the weighted linear model includes archetype, common dwelling type, location, the wave-specific insulation measure (subjective rating in 2013; recent improvements in 2021), age proxy, area proxy, cooling-system presence and the listed interactions. The 2021 specification was revised after the source-variable audit and is not a preregistered analysis. HC3 heteroscedasticity-robust covariance is used for joint coefficient-block Wald F tests on test households [10]. Main-effect block tests in a model containing interactions are conditional on its reference coding. They should not be read as total causal effects. Holm correction is applied across the available blocks within each response. The use of final survey weights does not make these full design-based survey tests. Cooling presence is reconstructed from the equipment inventory. Implications for building simulations The corrected outputs provide a way to represent uncertainty about the occupants of a dwelling. A simulator can draw an archetype from the four modeled probabilities and then use a compatible distribution of operating parameters. Repeated draws represent occupant-assignment uncertainty more faithfully than treating the most probable archetype as known. However, this study does not validate the resulting building-energy predictions. Any claimed reduction in simulation error requires a separate test using buildings with measured energy and comparable weather, systems and operation. For a regional or national stock model, the conditional sampling domain and weights matter. The present results do not cover households of six or more members, households with required missing education, or unknown dwelling categories in the probability stage. An operational implementation must state how it handles those cases and should not silently reuse the last available category. It should also preserve probability uncertainty instead of making deterministic assignments appear more certain than the evidence allows. 5.1. Its constants, 1717 kWh/year and 1879 kWh/year at 120 m², are reproduced here as calculations reported by that article [1], not newly verified universal requirements of the standards. The public ISO scope supports the interpretation of schedules as informative examples [8]. The original derivations and appliance boundaries would have to be independently checked against the complete standards before treating either as a like-for-like benchmark. Assumptions on heating-system schedules The original discussion compares reported heating hours with maximum daily durations associated with climatic zones. A maximum duration is not an estimate of typical use. The corrected Table 8 therefore treats the values 6, 8, 10, 12 and 14 hours as explicit schedule scenarios quoted from the source paper, not as current legal advice or household-level compliance classifications. Conclusions The 2021 release supports explicit analyses of cooling ownership, summer operating duration and recent envelope improvements. Including the renovation indicator restores the corresponding heating-model tests and stratified distributions. Its interpretation remains recent investment activity rather than insulation quality or measured energy savings. Cooling-use estimates describe frequent users in summer 2020; extending them to annual demand requires additional modeling. This corrected 2021 analysis retains a four-archetype framework while replacing the source implementation's recursive education recoding, undocumented age distortions and hard-coded component expressions. The reference model is based on a documented eligible sample and a fitted weighted PCA. The 2021 households are assigned through the unchanged 2013 reference, allowing the meaning of a label to remain fixed rather than being recreated independently. The dwelling model yields a valid four-category probability distribution by construction. Its test Brier score of 0.7071, compared with 0.7289 for the marginal baseline, provides a direct assessment of predictive value under the proxy assumptions. Neither high in-sample R² nor visual calibration alone is used to claim household-level accuracy. The secondary location/type model and sparse-cell diagnostics remain available for comparison, without a claim that all omitted variables are irrelevant. The training-and-tuning process selects 8 heating groups. Their test prediction error is 4.76 hours, while the constant training predictor has error 5.08 hours. Group memberships are not repeatedly modified to obtain significant test differences. The 2021 heating distributions are stratified by recent insulation/window improvements. Appliance electricity is modeled under reference 2013 parameters and explicit assumptions; cooling operating hours and ownership provide separate observed results. Three limitations remain unresolved by code corrections alone. First, non-nested age bands and combined education categories prevent exact measurement equivalence between releases; the scenario appendix shows the resulting sensitivity. Second, the uncertainty calculations do not reconstruct the full survey design. Third, no independent building-simulation validation has been performed. The manuscripts therefore support a reproducible, explicitly conditional reanalysis, not a claim that all original conclusions have been confirmed or that the revised framework is ready for adoption as a standard. Cross-year differences require additional restraint because the later heating answers refer to pandemic-era winter 2020 and the surveys differ in frame and mode. Even with a fixed archetype model and expansion weights, changes can combine demographic composition, measurement, selection and period effects. The paired release is useful for transfer testing and methodological comparison, but does not identify a causal temporal change in occupant behavior. Appendix A.
