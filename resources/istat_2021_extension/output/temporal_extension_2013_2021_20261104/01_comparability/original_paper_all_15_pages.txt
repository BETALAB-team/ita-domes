ABSTRACT
Traditional building energy simulations often rely on generalized assumptions about user-related variables, 
leading to discrepancies between predicted and actual energy consumption. This study addresses this challenge 
by proposing a data-driven approach to identify and incorporate user archetypes into building energy simula -
tions based on responses to a national survey. Focusing on the Italian residential sector, the study analyzes a 
comprehensive dataset to uncover distinct patterns in occupant demographics, building characteristics, and 
energy use patterns. Applying unsupervised clustering techniques, four distinct user archetypes emerged, 
characterized by demographic features like age, household size, and education level. The analysis shows a 
correlation between building characteristics and the probability of specific user archetypes inhabiting those 
buildings. Notably, building age and size emerged as strong predictors of archetype occupancy. Further analysis 
revealed that user archetype, in conjunction with specific dwelling characteristics, significantly influences energy 
consumption patterns. Daily heating system usage and annual electricity consumption varied considerably across 
user archetypes and building types. Integrating these findings into Standards or technical literature would 
enhance the accuracy of simulation results where there is a lack of information about residents, enabling targeted 
energy efficiency strategies and contributing to a more sustainable built environment.
1. Introduction
Energy efficiency in buildings is increasingly recognized as a 
cornerstone of sustainable development [ 1 ]. Global energy demand 
rises, and reducing energy consumption has become a critical focus for 
policymakers. Buildings, with their huge part in this demand [ 2 ], have 
become a key target for achieving climate goals [ 3 , 4 ]. However, the 
simulations attempting to inspect the energy performance of the build -
ings are often far from what happens inside the buildings. Understand -
ing and accurately predicting energy consumption in buildings requires 
a multifaceted approach that considers not only physical and technical 
characteristics but also human behavior [ 5 , 6 ]. Occupants play a central 
role in determining actual energy use through their daily activities, 
preferences, and interactions with building systems [ 7 – 9 ]. Studies show 
that discrepancies between predicted and actual energy performance 
can often be attributed to variations in user behavior in heating, cooling, 
and appliance usage patterns [ 10 ].
1.1. International standards
European Standard EN 16798 [ 11 ] sets out environmental, lighting 
and acoustic comfort parameters for design conditions and energy 
assessment. The informative Appendix C of the standard lists the pa -
rameters and setpoints with the relevant use profiles. The environments 
considered are: school, nursery, department store, meeting room, office, 
single office, restaurant, residential flat and single-family residential 
house.
For each type of environment, the time of use, internal loads and 
setpoints are defined. For internal loads, occupancy density, thermal 
loads due to occupants, thermal loads due to equipment and lighting, 
and steam and carbon dioxide production are specified. For set-points, 
values are defined for operating temperature, ventilation rate, relative 
humidity and carbon dioxide concentration. The profiles are defined in 
hourly steps, distinguishing weekdays from weekends.
Concerning the residential sector, EN 16798 covers two categories: 
the flat and the single-family house. For the flat, the standard also dis -
tinguishes according to the end user: a flat inhabited by elderly people

ISO 18,523 provides detailed use profiles for simulations for calcu -
lating the energy needs of buildings. The standard defines parameters 
related to internal loads and the operation of a specific thermal zone

Part 2 of ISO 18523 provides less detailed information than Part 1 for 
non-residential buildings, as the definition of ratings and time profiles

variability that can occur. However, the information present makes it 
possible to define reference profiles that can be used for energy 
simulations.
Rugani et al [ 14 ] performed a sensitivity analysis to investigate the 
impact of different assumptions for occupancy profiles coming from 
different sources. Depending on the climate considered, differences in 
the resulting heating demand go from 8 % to 16 % for detached houses 
and between 20 % and 40 % for apartments. The impact of occupancy 
assumptions is even higher for cooling demands, the error being 15 % to 
40 % for detached houses and 42 % to 142 % for apartments. This issue 
shows the possibility of a catastrophic deviation from reality in case the 
occupants assigned to the buildings are unknown; a situation that arises 
when the residents are not defined in the project, or in large-scale 
simulations where such information is not available.
1.2. Literature review
There is often a significant discrepancy between design and real 
energy use in buildings. While the design use derives from calculations 
based on assumptions, the latter is influenced by six factors: climate, 
building envelope, building services and energy systems, building 
operation and maintenance, occupants’ activities and behavior, and 
indoor environmental quality [ 3 ]. Numerous studies have shown that 
buildings even when designed to high-efficiency standards may 
consume differently than what is anticipated. The user-related factors 
often determine whether a building achieves its intended performance 
[ 15 ]. Occupant behavior, in particular, is a central driver of actual en -
ergy consumption, and is typically defined as a set of observable actions 
or reactions taken in response to external or internal environmental 
stimuli – for instance, switching on lights when daylight becomes 
insufficient [ 16 ]. Because detalied behavioral data is often unavailable, 
researchers frequently assess behavior through indirect indicators [ 17 ] 
such as total electricity consumption or average daily heating demand. 
This study also keeps this approach to propose a method that can be used 
in large-scale building simulations.
The lack of understanding about the user attributes in energy anal -
ysis of new buildings or retrofit scenarios leads to an energy gap [ 18 ]. A 
gap between simulation and reality, which can be linked either to the 
rebound effect, i.e. to the tendency of occupants to increase their energy 
demand for comfort when household energy efficiency is enhanced [ 19 ] 
or to the use of the building by a type of user unknown to the engineers 
doing the simulations [ 20 ].
The diversity of occupants’ behavior emerged in the study by Vivian 
et al. [ 21 ], in which the analysis of indoor air monitored before and after 
the renovation of a building suggests that the energy performance gap is 
determined by increased thermal comfort and improved air quality and 
that users tend to favour one or the other aspect.
In both cases, people’s views, habits, practices and resources deter -
mine how energy is consumed. social factors interweave with technical 
factors in buildings’ operation, such that the two often co-determine 
each other [ 22 ]. This diversity highlights the necessity to develop and 
study the user’s behavior and measures to assign them when investi -
gating building energy performance. Currently, studies are being carried 
out to dynamically introduce user preferences to the systems [ 23 ].
A growing body of literature is underlining that building energy 
simulations are often based on generalized assumptions [ 24 ]. The sim -
ulations usually overlook the diversity of building users and typically do 
not reflect the variation across households [ 25 ]. On this note, studies 
show that incorrect, misplaced or oversimplified profiles can cause es -
timates that are substantially deviated from reality [ 16 ]. Different 
studies measured this deviation based on occupant type assumption and 
occupant behavior modelling to be significantly high for both residential 
and non-residential cases [ 24 , 26 ].
Occupants’ behavior not only affects the energy efficiency of build -
ings but can also enhance or undermine their energy flexibility [ 27 , 28 ], 
which is an important asset to decarbonize the building sector in 
renewable-dominated energy systems, where the energy demand must 
adapt to the availability of renewable energy production.
The need for structured approaches to the collection, storage, 
sharing, and analysis of monitored data relevant to building occupants 
became a key focus in the discussions of Annex 66 and Annex 79 of the 
IEA TCP on Energy Buildings and Communities [ 29 ]. These research 
collaboration programs highlighted the challenges of comparing 
different studies due to the variety of methods and data ontologies used 
[ 29 ]. The issue’s significance is raised when a whole group of buildings 
are being dealt with. As an example, in the case of consumers, it is 
important to know the electricity usage of the users connected to the 
grid to analyze and control the system [ 30 ]. To investigate the perfor -
mance of a built environment at a city level, it is imperative to have an 
archetypical set of data to apply in the model. Such diverse profiles have 
been assessed in the field of design [ 31 ] using questionnaires and self- 
assessments.
Dong et al [ 32 ] introduced a database of 34 field-measured building 
occupant behavior datasets collected from 15 countries and 39 in -
stitutions across 10 climatic zones covering various building types in 
both commercial and residential sectors. The database includes occu -
pancy patterns (i.e., presence and people count) and occupant behaviors 
(i.e., interactions with devices, equipment, and technical systems in 
buildings).
This study takes an extensive statistical approach for processing and 
classifying the data derived from a national energy survey (discussed in 
Section 2 ). This database is chosen to be the only information input for 
this study as it only covers the residential sector. This article focuses on 
the residential sector, as including other types of buildings might 
confound the data and the study on other types of buildings needs its 
own time and space. A previous study [ 33 ] used this dataset to generate 
energy consumption profiles based on the building types. While this 
study uses a conventional energy approach to derive profiles in a higher 
temporal resolution, it does not cover demographic aspects of the 
dataset. This dataset was previously used to develop a bottom-up na -
tional building stock model called MOIRAE [ 34 ]. The simulation tool 
was later improved and released with the name MODENA [ 35 ], which 
allowed to perform a sensitivity analysis to study the effect of climate 
change on future energy consumption [ 36 ].

1.3. Research gap and objectives
A persistent and widely recognized challenge in building energy 
modelling is the performance gap—the often substantial difference be -
tween predicted energy consumption in simulations and the actual 
measured use [ 16 ]. One of the main contributors to this gap is the use of 
oversimplified assumptions about building occupants, such as fixed 
schedules or average constant values for setpoints, occupancy, ventila -
tion rates, internal gains etc. Therefore, building simulations often rely 
on generalized assumptions coming from Standards that, in the best 
case, represent average national statistics and fail to catch the diversity 
among users, and in the worst case, are based on an unspecified “com -
mon sense”. As such, simulations become case-study dependent and 
prevents the reproducibility of the simulation results. This lack of a 
standardized methodology is one of the root causes of the observed 
discrepancies between predicted and actual energy consumption in 
buildings [ 37 ].
This study addresses this challenge by proposing a data-driven 
approach to identify and incorporate user archetypes into building en -
ergy simulations based on responses to an Italian national survey. The 
latter are available on an open dataset. The novelty of this work is the 
methodology to derive statistically representative users that can be used 
for building simulations based on the mentioned dataset. The method 
aims at answering to three research questions: (i) can a limited number 
of user archetypes be determined based on demographic features of the 
population?, (ii) is there any relation between user archetypes and the 
dwellings they inhabit?, (iii) is it possible to find energy use patterns 
depending on the user archetype and building type?. A thorough 
description of the techniques used to answer these research questions, 
and the results achieved for each of them, can be found in Sections 3 and 
4 , respectively. Section 5 discusses the relevance of the findings by 
showing the difference between values recommended from laws or In -
ternational Standards and those emerging from this analysis. Finally, 
Section 6 summarizes the main conclusions.
2. Dataset
This work relies on a dataset gathering detailed statistics on Italian 
households’ energy consumption [ 38 ]. This initiative was part of a 
broader effort to meet European Commission standards, as mandated by 
EU Regulation No. 1099/2008, to enhance energy-related statistics. 
Such data was necessary to guide both national and EU policy on energy 
efficiency and renewable energy. The target population comprised 
households with a landline subscription, selected randomly from Italy’s 
8,000 municipalities and stratified by demographics and geography (e. 
g., metropolitan centers, mountain towns, and coastal areas). The sam -
pling was one-stage, including a base sample of 20,000 households with 
a reserve sample of 100,000 additional households to ensure sufficient 
participation. Surveys were conducted between March and July 2013 
using CATI (Computer-Assisted Telephone Interviewing), and in -
terviews were monitored for data quality. Households and respondents 
were selected carefully, targeting an adult in each household knowl -
edgeable about energy topics. Although the data was collected in 2013, 
the key demographic factors used in this study, such as household size, 
age, and education, have not changed drastically over time. While some 
technological trends, such as the adoption of newer heating systems, 
more efficient appliances, or changes in telecommunication habits, may 
have evolved, the dataset remains a detailed and structured source 
available for analyzing household characteristics in Italy, making it well- 
suited for this analysis.
The survey includes more than 380 questions divided into nine 
sections: general information on households, household characteristics, 
heating system, domestic hot water, cooling system, biomass, lighting, 
household appliances, and energy expenditure. These questions concern 
the presence, type, characteristics, number and type of use of different 
technologies related to the energy consumption of the households 
surveyed. Using the answers to these questions, it was therefore possible 
to characterize the energy consumption of households by cross- 
referencing the answers with external databases or models that define 
consumption.
Data from the survey were cleaned and validated through deter -
ministic and probabilistic correction methods. Errors were identified 
using ISTAT’s CONCORD software, which helped ensure coherence 
across responses. Weights were calculated to adjust for representative -
ness, aligning survey estimates with demographic statistics on factors 
like household income, employment status, and regional distribution 
[ 39 ]. These weights enabled extrapolation to the broader population, 
enhancing the data’s policy relevance. Having these factors, it is possible 
to extend the results of this study to the national scale.
3. Methods
In this study, statistical data analysis is conducted on the dataset to 
investigate the possibility of relating family members’ information, 
building information, and household energy patterns. The systematic 
procedure employed in this study can be consulted in Fig. 1 . The analysis 
applies Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to the dataset to reduce its 
dimensionality and highlight the significant variables. This step was 
taken to uncover patterns in the data and simplify the clustering process 
[ 40 ]. By transforming the original data into orthogonal components, 
PCA leads to isolating the primary factors that distinguish different 
family types. Then, clustering algorithms [ 41 ] were used on the PCA- 
transformed data to group families based on shared characteristics.
Once ethe clusters are created based on the demographic data, it is 
necessary to find a way to link the user types to the buildings, or maybe 
to say there is no link. The first attempt in this study was to use classi -
fication techniques to predict the user archetype residing in a building 
based on construction properties. This approach proved ineffective 
using traditional classification models (specify) as there seems to be no 
explicit and certain connection between the type of families and the 
dwellings in which they reside. Instead, probabilistic feature extraction 
(PFE) methods [ 42 ] enabled estimation of the likelihood for each family 
type residing in specific building types.
After assigning family clusters and their probabilistic connection 
with their residential dwellings, an Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was 
conducted [ 43 ] to determine whether the combination of family clusters 
and dwelling characteristics can result in distinct groups to predict the 
energy consumption behavior of each unit.
3.1. PCA and clustering
Given the extensive number of variables available in the dataset, a 
preliminary selection was necessary to focus the analysis on household 
demographic characteristics rather than building features or equipment 
details. The primary objective of the study was to define household user 
archetypes based on their demographic composition. Therefore, vari -
ables describing physical aspects of the dwelling (such as heating sys -
tems, window materials, or appliance ownership) were excluded, as they 
were not directly informative about household structure. Instead, four 
variables were selected to capture the essential dimensions of household 
composition. These variables are summarized in Table 1 .
After calculating the selected household indicators, Principal 
Component Analysis (PCA) was applied to reduce the dimensionality of 
the dataset while explaining most of its variance. The aim of PCA was to 
identify a smaller set of uncorrelated components that summarize the 
main patterns within the household demographic data.
After defining the principal components and isolating the education 
variable, a clustering analysis was conducted to identify groups of 
similar households. The clustering approach chosen was the k-means 
algorithm, widely used for its simplicity and effectiveness in partitioning 
observations based on similarity. Before clustering, all variables (two 
principal components PC1, PC2, and education) were linearly normal -

The k-means algorithm operates by assigning each observation to the 
nearest cluster centroid based on a distance metric. In this study, the 
standard Euclidean distance was used to measure the proximity between 
data points and centroids. The optimization objective was to minimize 
the Within-Cluster Sum of Squares ( WCSS ), which quantifies the 
compactness of the clusters. The elbow point method is a technique used 
to determine the optimal number of clusters in clustering algorithms, by 
plotting the performance indicator, here WCSS against the number of 
clusters. The “ elbow ” in the plot, where the rate of improvement sharply 
decreases, indicates the ideal number of clusters to use.
To determine the optimal number of clusters, the Calinski-Harabasz 
Index ( CHI ) was used as a validation metric [ 45 ]. The CHI evaluates the 
clustering quality by comparing the dispersion within clusters to the 
dispersion between clusters. A higher Calinski-Harabasz index indicates 
better-defined clusters, with typical values above 100 suggesting a 
strong clustering structure, while values below 10 often indicate poorly 
defined clusters or inadequate grouping. Although the specific thresh -
olds can vary depending on the dataset and context, the index is handy 
for comparing clustering solutions and identifying the optimal number 
of clusters.
To assess whether regional differences had a significant influence on 
the identified household clusters, a multivariate analysis of variance 
(MANOVA) was conducted [ 46 ]. Unlike separate univariate ANOVA 
tests, MANOVA simultaneously examines multiple dependent variables, 
accounting for potential correlations among them and providing a more 
robust assessment of group differences.
In this analysis, the dependent variables were the two principal 
components ( PC 1 and PC 2) and the education variable, while the inde -
pendent variable was the household ’ s region of residence. Two test 
statistics were used to evaluate the significance of regional effects: 
Wilks ’ Lambda, which measures the proportion of total variance not 
explained by the grouping factor, and Roy ’ s Greatest Root, which 
highlights the largest eigenvalue associated with the group differences.
If the null hypothesis was rejected based on these tests, it would 
suggest that the region significantly impacts the variance of the de -
mographic structure and education level across clusters, indicating the 
necessity to consider regional factors in interpreting the clustering 
results.
3.2. Probabilistic feature extraction
After grouping the families into clusters, the second objective is to 
find a way to connect the dwelling information to the family informa -
tion. Now that the residents can be described using a categorical vari -
able, a classic way to tackle such a problem would be to train a 
classification model that can use the information of the dwellings to 
predict the type of families living inside. Such approach was tested in 
this study. However, in the preliminary inspection, the resulting models 
did not yield acceptable outcomes in terms of confusion matirx of the 
classification algorithm. This issue ruled out the classification method as 
a suitable tool for assigning family types to the dwellings.
That is why the study investigated the possibility of predicting the 
probability of having a specific family type in a dwelling. To this end, A 
probabilistic feature extraction (PFE) was conducted on the dataset 
[ 47 ].

3.3. Regression model analysis and analysis of variance
A regression model was applied to the dataset to explore the effect of 
building information and user archetype on two selected variables 
related to occupants behavior, namely the declared number of hours per 
day in which the heating system is on, and the annual electrical con -
sumption from the appliances, calculated with the model [ 35 ] based on 
the answers to the survey. Considering the high number of multi-class 
categorical variables, a multi-step ANOVA was used to investigate 
their influence on energy behavior.
ANOVA (Analysis of Variance) was used to find the significance of 
user and building type in energy consumption behaviors. The analysis of 
the effect of user archetypes and building type on the mentioned 
behavioral variables started with an exploratory analysis. The study 
employs an iterative approach to grouping categories based on statisti -
cal similarity using Tukey ’ s Honest Significant Difference (HSD) test 
[ 48 ]. Algorithm 2 shows the data preparation, statistical testing, and 
group merging process used to identify the significance of categorical 
variables in the response variable. Tukey HSD test is a post-hoc analysis 
to perform pairwise comparisons between group means while control -
ling the Family-wise Error Rate (FWER). The test uses the studentized 
range distribution to compare the differences between group means 
against a critical threshold. This helps identify which groups differ 
significantly, controlling the probability of Type I error across all com -
parisons. However, considering the large number of categorical levels in 
different datasets, an iterative approach can be used in multiple steps to 
identify a significant set of categories.
This process systematically reduces the number of groups, noting 
that the steps 2 – 5 will decrease the number of groups by exactly one in 
each iteration, while preserving statistically meaningful distinctions in 
the response variable across categorical combinations.
Algorithm 2 multi-step pairwise grouping
Input: A dataset including a set of categorical variables X and a numerical response 
variable Y
Output: a subset of all possible combinations of X that make most significant effect on 
Y
1. Perform ANOVA on the dataset, using interaction terms among all categorical 
factors
2. Conduct the Tukey HSD test on all pairwise group combinations to obtain 
the p-values
3. Identify the pairs of groups with the highest p-values (least significance 
differences)
4. If p-values exceed a threshold, merge the corresponding groups into a new 
group
5. Update the dataset by replacing the old group labels with the new merged 
group labels
6. Repeat step 2 – 5 until all remaining groups show statistically significant 
differences.
7. Record the final groups for the analysis

5. Implications for building simulations
Although some trends in the results discussed above are obvious, 
such as the dependence of heating hours on floor area, thermal insu -
lation and climate, the role of building occupants and their interaction 
with different buildings located in various climate zones is an open 
research topic.
The research gap in this area is mainly due to the complexity of 
dealing with occupant behavior. This research topic lies at the inter -
section of several disciplines, such as building physics, behavioral psy -
chology, and statistics. The latter is particularly relevant because 
analyzing user behavior must necessarily start from observed empirical 
data. Furthermore, different users located in countries with different 
socio-economic, climatic and cultural characteristics must necessarily be 
considered differently.
For all these reasons, today’s building energy simulation Standards 
do not provide the required information to account for the diversity of 
users. For instance, EN 16798 and ISO 18523 do not provide any 
quantitative or methodological information to address users’ diversity. 
These standards rely on fixed values for variables related to occupancy, 
internal heat gains, and system schedules, assuming a homogeneous 
distribution in user behavior. The analysis demonstrates that such ap -
proximations lead to inaccuracies in energy consumption predictions. 
For instance, Rugani et al. found that heating demand differences based 
on varying occupancy profiles ranged from 20 % to 40 % for apartments 
[ 14 ].
The standards’ reliance on single or narrowly defined values for user 
behavior parameters is a critical shortcoming. For instance, to reiterate 
what was discussed in the introduction, EN 16798 recognizes only three 
residential archetypes (single-family houses, flats resided by elderly 
people and flats resided by non-elderly people), while ISO 18523 con -
siders a prototypical household with a standard family consisting of a 
couple of parents with two young children. These assumptions do not 
have an empirical basis and may be influenced by gender bias or pre -
vailing opinions within the standardization committees.
To address these limitations, this study introduces a statistical 
method to identify the distinct user archetypes based on demographic 
information. By incorporating probabilistic models, this approach links 
these archetypes to specific building characteristics. This connection 
will make the process of energy simulation in buildings more reliable, as 
with the building characteristics, it is possible to understand the prob -
ability of different user types residing in that dwelling. Having linked the 
building to the user type, in the next phase, different significant groups 
have been found that show similar behaviors. The integration of user 
archetypes into building energy simulations provides several 
advantages: 
– enhanced accuracy: the proposed approach significantly reduces the 
prediction errors inherent in current standards by replacing fixed 
values with archetype-specific probabilities and behavioral patterns.
– improved representativeness: the clustering process ensures that 
simulations reflect the diversity of user behavior across different 
demographic and geographic contexts.
– policy relevance: probabilistic predictions enable policymakers and 
designers to target specific user groups with tailored energy effi -
ciency measures, maximizing the impact of interventions.

5.2. Assumptions on heating systems schedule
Regarding the hours of on-time for the heating systems, in the Italian 
context, the simulations are normally done taking the values given in 
DPR 412 [ 49 ] as the working hours. These values are based on the cli -
matic zones assigned to different geographical regions of the country. 
These zones are defined based on the value of degree days related to 
each area. Fig. 10 shows the mentioned climatic zones and their 
respective daily limit for the heating system working hours.
A comparison between this figure and the information in Table 6
demonstrates the issue that arises when assuming the limiting maximum 
hour as the reference value. Many of the dwellings do not consume as 
much as the maximum allowable hours. An apartment in northeastern 
Italy, for instance, in this study, is assigned to one of the significant 
groups, H4, H5, or H6. Their corresponding average working time for 
the heating system are 7.53, 8.53, and 9.14 h per day. On the other hand, 
this apartment can be in climatic zones D, E, or F, which means that its 
heating system is considered active at least 12 h a day in the effective 
current simulations. Furthermore, the user type and its effect would be 
neglected. In contrast, the results of this study show that, for instance, an 
older couple might keep the heating system on longer than a younger 
family. A statement that can be justified by the previous studies [51] on 
the effect of age on the feeling of thermal comfort. Table 8 shows the 
error in the heating system ’ s assumed number of operating hours based 
on the climatic zone instead of using the survey data. As seen, the as -
sumptions based on the standards show high differences for most cases, 
which can reach up to 114 %.

6. Conclusions
This study identified four user archetypes through unsupervised 
clustering, highlighting the diversity of occupant behavior in the Italian 
residential sector. The archetypes are characterized by variations in 
household demographics, including age, family size, and education 
level. While the dataset used in this study is relatively old at the time this 
study is conducted, the methodology can be applied to any similar 
dataset, making it possible to obtain user archetypes and their energy 
consumption patterns whenever and wherever similar information is 
available.
By analyzing building area and age, the study successfully estab -
lished a probabilistic relationship between dwelling characteristics and 
the likelihood of specific user archetypes inhabiting them. The proba -
bilistic feature extraction approach captured nuanced correlations, 
revealing that larger and older buildings are more likely to house certain 
users, while smaller and newer buildings correspond to others. This 
linkage hints at the interplay between physical attributes of dwellings 
and occupant demographics, offering a predictive tool to enhance the 
integration of user archetypes into energy simulations.
While dwelling-related variables such as region, insulation level, and 
dwelling type were included in the analysis, they did not exhibit sig -
nificant direct correlations with user archetypes. This finding highlights 
the limited predictive power of these variables in determining occupant 
behavior compared to demographic factors like age, and area of the 
dwelling. However, the study acknowledges the potential of these 
dwelling characteristics to influence energy-related behaviors when 
considered in conjunction with user archetypes.
The combined influence of user archetypes and specific dwelling- 
related variables, such as dwelling type, insulation level, building 
area, and building age, significantly affects energy-related behaviors. 
The analysis identified seven significant groups for heating behavior, 
shaped by the interaction of location, user archetype, and dwelling type 
alongside the level of the building ’ s thermal insulation. Similarly, six 
significant groups were found for electricity consumption, influenced by 
location, dwelling type, presence of the cooling system, and user 
archetype.
This study highlights the importance of integrating user archetypes 
into building energy simulations to improve prediction accuracy and 
facilitate targeted energy efficiency strategies. By identifying four 
distinct archetypes and associating them with both occupant de -
mographics and dwelling characteristics, the research bridges the gap 
between behavioral diversity and physical building features.
In conclusion, this study provides a methodological basis for iden -
tifying user archetypes that can be embedded into national Standards on 
building simulations to improve the precision assigning user type and 
user behavior parameters to buildings. To this purpose, future studies 
will investigate the impact of user archetypes on the outcome of indi -
vidual building energy simulations. Moreover, access to the updated 
ISTAT surveys (that has been repeated for 2021 and 2024 but the results 
are unavailable at the time of this study) will make it possible to study 
the temporal evolution of the energy consumption patterns in the Italian 
residential sector.
